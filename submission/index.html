A field guide to the blue dot

Nobody up there knows where you are

The blue dot on your map isn’t something a satellite tells your phone. No satellite knows where you are, or that you exist. About thirty of them, 20,200 km up,1 do one thing over and over: they say which satellite they are, where they are, and what time it is by the atomic clock on board. Your phone only listens. From how late each message arrives, it works out where it must be standing, about once a second, usually to within a few metres.2

Sheet 1 Where the blue dot comes from

Fig. 1. The Earth and the satellites to scale. Around each satellite you can hear is a circle as big as the distance its signal says it travelled. You are wherever the circles meet. Drag a satellite along its orbit, click one to stop listening to it, or drag you around the Earth.

Fig. 2. The dots are the answers the phone would give over many seconds, because each measured distance is a few metres off in a different way each time.

Radio travels at the speed of light, 300,000 km a second, so a satellite’s message takes between about 67 and 86 thousandths of a second to reach you. Your phone compares the time in the message with the time it arrived, and that tells it how far away the satellite is. One distance puts you somewhere on a circle around that satellite. Two circles cross in two places. A third picks one.

That only works if your phone’s clock is as good as the satellites’, and it isn’t.3 A clock a millionth of a second out makes every distance 300 metres wrong. So the phone treats its own clock error as one more unknown and uses one more satellite to find it. Every distance is too long by the same amount, and only one amount makes all the circles meet in a point.

How the phone worked it out

The phone can’t solve this in one go. It guesses, works out how late each signal would have been if the guess were right, and moves the guess to shrink the difference.4

    Sheet 2 Why the dot wanders downtown

    Everything on Sheet 1 assumes each signal comes straight from the satellite. Between tall buildings, many don’t. This is a street seen end on, with glass on both sides. Stand still and let the sky turn: the satellites move,5 different signals get blocked or bounce, and the dot on the map wanders even though you haven’t moved.

    Fig. 3. Blue: straight from the satellite. Orange: bounced off the glass opposite, so it took the long way round. Grey: blocked. Click the street to move where you stand.

    Fig. 4. Your phone’s map. The fading trail is where the dot has been.

    A bounced signal arrives late, because it went to the far wall and back. The phone can’t tell it bounced. It only sees a satellite that seems further away than it is, and it pushes your dot away from that satellite to make the circles meet. A signal that bounced off the building on your left came from a satellite on your right, so the dot slides left, often into the building.

    The obvious fix is to ignore satellites low in the sky, but they are the ones that tell left from right. With only the satellites straight overhead, the circles cross at a shallow angle and the pale circle grows wide across the street. Make the buildings taller and watch both problems get worse together.

    Newer phones use a 3D map of the city instead.6 The phone already knows it’s at street level and roughly which street it’s in. So it tries standing at each spot across the street, uses the map to predict how late every signal should be from there, bounces included, and picks the spot where the predictions fit. Flip the switch to try it. Our map is perfect and our walls are flat glass, so it works better here than it does in a real city.

    Notes

    1. The US Space Force has kept about 31 GPS satellites working in recent years, spread over six orbits, each going round in just under 12 hours. Phones also listen to Europe’s Galileo, Russia’s GLONASS and China’s BeiDou, which work the same way. ↩
    2. The US government says phones are typically accurate to within 4.9 metres under open sky. Every number on this page is worked out in your browser by about 900 lines of our own JavaScript, with no libraries. The code is on GitHub. ↩
    3. The satellites carry atomic clocks, and even those need care: because gravity is weaker up there and they move fast, they would run about 38 millionths of a second a day fast compared with clocks on the ground. So before launch they are set to tick slightly slow, 10.22999999543 MHz instead of 10.23 MHz, and Einstein does the rest. ↩
    4. This is Gauss–Newton least squares, the standard way receivers do it. Starting from the centre of the Earth is a common first guess when the phone has no idea where it is, and it still gets there in about five steps. Real phones usually start from their last answer, or from the nearest phone mast. ↩
    5. We turn the sky about seventy times faster than real life. Each satellite also sends a description of its orbit, but only at 50 bits a second, so the full set takes 12.5 minutes to hear. That’s why your phone downloads it from the internet instead (“assisted GPS”), and why a dot can take a while to appear in the middle of nowhere. ↩
    6. This is called 3D-mapping-aided positioning. Android phones began using it in some big cities in 2020. A related method, shadow matching, uses which satellites seem to be hidden behind buildings, the shadows they cast in the radio sky. ↩
