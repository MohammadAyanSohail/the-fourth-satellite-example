The throat in your phone

Tech used
Plain JavaScript, HTML and CSS, with no libraries or build step. Web Audio only plays and records the sound. The voices were made with Google Gemini text-to-speech. Hosted on GitHub Pages.
What your project is about
A phone call has room for about 12,000 bits a second, and a voice doesn't fit. So the phone works out the shape of your throat and mouth 50 times a second and sends that, and the other phone builds a throat to match and blows through it. Our site is a working version of the simplest way to do it, the 1980s method today's phones grew out of. You can record yourself and hear what the other end gets, flip back to your real voice at the same moment, watch the throat move with each packet, and hear why hold music sounds so bad.
Who is it for?
Anyone from about 13 up who uses a phone and has never thought about what happens to their voice on the way. You hear each idea before you read about it, so there's no maths to get through first.
One-line summary
Hear your own voice the way a phone sends it: a throat shape, a pitch and a loudness, 50 times a second, rebuilt at the other end.
How it works
Every 20 ms the site takes a slice of sound and finds ten numbers that describe it as a tube of ten sections, using linear prediction and the Levinson–Durbin recursion. It also measures the pitch, the loudness, and whether the sound is a buzz or a hiss. Those four things are rounded to 54 bits, which at 50 packets a second comes to 2.7 kbit/s, close to LPC-10, the US standard from 1984. The other end rebuilds the voice by sending clicks at that pitch, or noise, through the tube. Phones today send the same throat with a smarter buzz, which we explain but don't build. All of it is our own JavaScript, about 1,800 lines, running in your browser.
Why it matters
Billions of calls a day work like this, and almost nobody knows their phone is rebuilding a voice. Once you've heard your own voice turned into ten numbers and a pitch, everyday things start to make sense: a bad line goes robotic instead of crackly, a whisper still gets through, and hold music turns to mush. It's also an idea that turns up all over engineering. When there isn't room to send something, you send a description good enough to rebuild it.
